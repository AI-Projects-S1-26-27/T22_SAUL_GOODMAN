How the judge decides

Who's in the room

Three seats, two strategies and one rulebook. The lawyers are AI agents that make choices. The judge never does: it only applies rules.

Judge

A rulebook, not an AI

Rules on every objection with a written rule, then weighs only the accepted evidence. The same record always gives the same verdict.

The record

Every move lands here and is fingerprinted, so any trial can be replayed and checked.

Prosecution

Has to prove every point

Chooses which evidence to show and when. One unproven point and the whole case fails. In civil cases this seat is the plaintiff.

Defense

Only has to break one point

Objects to flawed evidence, challenges witnesses and offers evidence of its own.

The catch: neither lawyer can see the other side's hidden flaws for sure. Each one has to guess whether a piece of evidence is second-hand, unverified or obtained illegally, and objecting wrongly costs a penalty.

What a lawyer can do on a turn

Five moves. Each one costs part of a fixed budget, so no side can do everything.

Present evidence
Offer a document, record or witness. The other side gets one chance to object.
Object
Claim the evidence is hearsay, unverified, illegally obtained, shared too late, guesswork or off-topic. Wrong objections are penalised.
Challenge a witness
Use evidence to show a witness can't be trusted. If it works, that witness counts for half.
Ask to end early
The defense can ask the judge to stop the trial if a point has no accepted evidence at all.
Rest
Stop presenting. Passing twice in a row counts as resting, so nobody can stall forever.

Four ways to play

You pick a style for each lawyer. The styles are fixed strategies, so you can compare them fairly.

  • Aggressive

    Offers everything that helps, even shaky evidence, and objects on thin suspicion.

  • Careful

    Offers only clean evidence that matters, and objects only when it's confident.

  • Adaptive

    Weighs the odds of every move and learns how often the other side objects.

  • Chaotic

    Makes random, often illegal moves. It exists to prove the rules can't be broken.

  • Exact fractions, not rounding. The judge compares exact numbers, so rounding can never flip a verdict.
  • Every ruling cites a rule. Objections, fines and early endings each point to a written rule.
  • Every step is fingerprinted. Each event is chained to the one before it, so any edit to the record shows.
  • Stress-tested. 2,500 trials with random and rule-breaking lawyers ran with no crashes, and every replay matched bit for bit.

                How the judge decides

                There's no AI in the judge's chair. It's a fixed set of rules written down in advance, so anyone can check why a trial ended the way it did.

                1. Evidence is offered

                  One side presents a document, record or witness. The other side can object if it's second-hand, obtained illegally, unverified, shared too late or off-topic.

                2. The judge rules

                  Each objection is accepted or rejected by a written rule. Evidence that's thrown out doesn't count. A failed objection costs a penalty, so objecting isn't free.

                3. The evidence is weighed

                  Reliable sources count for more. When two pieces of evidence contradict each other, both count for less. A discredited witness counts for half.

                4. Every point must clear the bar

                  Criminal cases need about 90% certainty on each point. Civil cases need more than 50%. If even one point falls short, the accused wins.

                How high the bar is

                The judge has to be at least this sure of every single point. Each case in the simulator uses one of these.

                Balance of probabilitiesCivil cases. More likely than not.

                > 50%

                Clear and convincingA middle bar for serious civil claims.

                about 75%

                Beyond reasonable doubtCriminal cases. The accused is presumed innocent.

                When an objection succeeds

                Flawed evidence only gets thrown out if the other side objects on the right ground. Unchallenged flaws slip through, which is why spotting them matters.

                Hearsay OBJ-HEARSAY
                Someone is repeating what they were told.
                Not verified OBJ-AUTH
                No one has shown the evidence is genuine.
                Obtained illegally OBJ-EXCLUSIONARY
                Applies to prosecution evidence in criminal cases. Knowingly offering it brings a heavier penalty.
                Shared too late OBJ-DISCLOSURE
                It wasn't handed over before the trial. Also a heavier penalty.
                Guesswork OBJ-SPECULATION
                A witness is testifying about something they didn't see first-hand.
                Off-topic OBJ-RELEVANCE
                The evidence barely touches any point in the case.

                Same case, same choices, same verdict, every time. Each step of the trial record is fingerprinted, so any change to it would show.

                The exact rule, per point e, over accepted evidence only

                S_i    = reliability × witness credibility (halved per successful challenge)
disc_i = min(9/10, Σ_{j contradicts i} (4/5)·S_j/(S_i+S_j))
corr   = 1 + ½(1 − 2^-k),  k = other facts supporting the same way
L_e    = prior + 3 · Σ support·S·(1−disc)·corr      (exact fractions)
verdict: guilty / liable only if every L_e clears the standard

                Bars: beyond reasonable doubt 11/5 log-odds (about 90%), clear and convincing 11/10 (about 75%), balance of probabilities > 0 (more than 50%).
